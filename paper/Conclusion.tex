\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have developed PULSUS, a direct frequency-domain framework for
spectroscopy of open quantum systems. The method combines
Liouville-space resolvents with operator-valued Gaussian pulse dressing,
allowing requested excitation and detection frequencies to be evaluated
directly without first constructing a multidimensional time-domain
interferogram. For third-order spectroscopy, the complete
light--matter interaction may be retained, while the finite-pulse RWA,
short-pulse RWA, and impulsive-RWA descriptions arise as controlled
reductions of the same formulation.

The finite-pulse implementation was tested through complementary
benchmarks. For the conventional rephasing sector, PULSUS reproduces
the analytical Gaussian-pulse benchmark of Smallwood \textit{et al.}
with a relative discrepancy of $7.33\times10^{-6}$ at
$T=2\pi\sigma$, with the residual decreasing as the pulse separation
is increased. The complete-interaction Model A was independently
compared with explicit time-domain perturbative propagation, yielding
spectral-shape discrepancies of $0.0586\%$ and $0.0582\%$ for the
nonrephasing and rephasing responses, respectively, at the common
converged transform window. Cartesian-grid and arbitrary-coordinate
evaluations additionally agree to numerical precision.

Treating spectral coordinates as first-class computational objects
provides a natural route to selective sampling. In the coupled-dimer
benchmark, concentrating the coordinate budget toward the spectrally
active region reduced the number of evaluated points required for
approximately $10\%$ reconstruction error by a factor of about $6.2$,
with a corresponding wall-clock reduction of about $1.8$. These gains
depend on the model and reconstruction procedure, but demonstrate that
direct evaluation can avoid computing large regions of frequency space
that are not required by the analysis.

The controlled hierarchy
$A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C\rightarrow D$ also provides a direct
diagnostic of the physical approximations entering a calculated
spectrum. For the coupled dimer, the RWA, in-pulse molecular dynamics,
and finite spectral bandwidth dominate in different regions of pulse
duration and coupling strength, producing a nonmonotonic total
impulsive-RWA error. In the ultrashort-pulse stress-test regime, the
dominant nonrephasing correction discarded by the RWA is traced to the
molecular-interaction sequence
$\mathbf{m}=(+,+,-)$, whose contribution grows with electronic
coupling. This illustrates that PULSUS can be used not only to quantify
the failure of an approximation, but also to identify the
molecular-interaction content responsible for it.

The current implementation provides first- and third-order response
calculations and, for the finite-pulse treatment developed here, assumes
a time-independent field-free Liouvillian, stationary preparation,
temporally separated pulses, and Gaussian pulse envelopes. Within this
scope, PULSUS is particularly useful when selected spectral coordinates
rather than complete delay grids are the desired computational objects,
when molecular evolution during finite Gaussian pulses must be retained,
or when the validity of RWA, short-pulse, and impulsive approximations
is itself part of the spectroscopic question. It therefore complements
more general pulse-shape- and pathway-oriented frameworks by providing
a compact direct-frequency-domain route in which spectral sampling and
physical approximations can be controlled explicitly within a common
response formulation.
