\section{Theory and Method}
\label{sec:theory}
The PULSUS formulation combines open-system Liouville-space dynamics,
direct frequency-domain resolvents, and finite-pulse operator dressing.
The overall construction is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:method_workflow}.
We first introduce the Liouville-space representation and the direct
third-order frequency-domain response, then define the impulsive and
rotating-wave limits, and finally construct a controlled hierarchy of
finite-pulse approximations.
\subsection{Liouville-Space Formulation}
\label{sec:liouville}

We consider a finite-dimensional open quantum system described by a
density operator $\rho$ and a time-independent system Hamiltonian $H$.
Liouville-space descriptions of dissipative multidimensional
spectroscopy provide a standard framework for incorporating relaxation
and dephasing into nonlinear response calculations
\cite{
Abramavicius2009CoherentMultidimensional,
SandaMukamel2008StochasticLiouville}.
In the absence of an external field, the dynamics are assumed to be
Markovian and are written in Lindblad form,
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\rho}{dt}
=
-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]
+
\sum_k
\left(
L_k \rho L_k^\dagger
-\frac{1}{2}
\left\{
L_k^\dagger L_k,\rho
\right\}
\right),
\label{eq:lindblad}
\end{equation}
%
where $L_k$ are jump operators describing dissipative and dephasing
processes.

To express the dynamics as an ordinary linear equation, we vectorize
the density operator using column-stacking. For a basis
$\{\ket{i}\}$,
%
\begin{equation}
\rho
=
\sum_{ij}
\rho_{ij}
\ket{i}\bra{j},
\end{equation}
%
we define the corresponding Liouville-space vector
%
\begin{equation}
|\rho\rangle\rangle
\equiv
\operatorname{vec}(\rho).
\label{eq:vectorization}
\end{equation}
%
The column-stacking convention obeys
%
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{vec}(A\rho B)
=
\left(
B^{T}\otimes A
\right)
|\rho\rangle\rangle .
\label{eq:vec_identity}
\end{equation}
%
The field-free equation of motion can therefore be written as
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}
|\rho(t)\rangle\rangle
=
\mathcal{L}
|\rho(t)\rangle\rangle ,
\label{eq:liouville_eom}
\end{equation}
%
with Liouvillian
%
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}
={}&
-\frac{i}{\hbar}
\left(
I\otimes H
-
H^{T}\otimes I
\right)
\nonumber\\
&+
\sum_k
\left[
L_k^{*}\otimes L_k
-\frac{1}{2}
I\otimes L_k^\dagger L_k
-\frac{1}{2}
\left(
L_k^\dagger L_k
\right)^{T}\otimes I
\right].
\label{eq:liouvillian}
\end{align}
%
For a time-independent Liouvillian, the free evolution is simply
%
\begin{equation}
|\rho(t)\rangle\rangle
=
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
|\rho(0)\rangle\rangle .
\label{eq:free_liouville_propagation}
\end{equation}

Expectation values are represented through the Hilbert--Schmidt inner
product,
%
\begin{equation}
\langle\langle A|B\rangle\rangle
=
\operatorname{Tr}
\left(
A^\dagger B
\right),
\label{eq:hilbert_schmidt}
\end{equation}
%
so that the expectation value of an observable $A$ is
%
\begin{equation}
\langle A(t)\rangle
=
\langle\langle A|\rho(t)\rangle\rangle .
\label{eq:observable_liouville}
\end{equation}

Within the electric-dipole approximation, interaction with a classical
electric field $E(t)$ is described by
%
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{int}}(t)
=
-\mu E(t),
\label{eq:dipole_interaction}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mu$ is the molecular dipole operator. We define the associated
light--matter interaction superoperator
%
\begin{equation}
V
=
\frac{i}{\hbar}
\left(
I\otimes\mu
-
\mu^{T}\otimes I
\right),
\label{eq:interaction_superoperator}
\end{equation}
%
such that the complete driven equation of motion becomes
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}
|\rho(t)\rangle\rangle
=
\left[
\mathcal{L}
+
E(t)V
\right]
|\rho(t)\rangle\rangle .
\label{eq:driven_liouville}
\end{equation}
%
This separation between field-free evolution generated by
$\mathcal{L}$ and light--matter interactions generated by $V$ provides
the starting point for constructing the nonlinear response directly
in the frequency domain.

\subsection{Direct Frequency-Domain Response}
\label{sec:direct_frequency}

Starting from Eq.~\eqref{eq:driven_liouville}, we treat the
light--matter interaction perturbatively. For three chronologically
ordered interactions, the third-order molecular response can be written
in Liouville space as
%
\begin{equation}
R^{(3)}(t_3,T,t_1)
=
\langle\!\langle \mu |
e^{\mathcal{L}t_3}
V
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
V
e^{\mathcal{L}t_1}
V
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle ,
\label{eq:third_order_time}
\end{equation}
%
where $t_1$ and $t_3$ are the first and third coherence intervals,
respectively, and $T$ is the waiting time. Causality requires
$t_1,t_3\geq 0$.

In a conventional time-domain calculation,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:third_order_time} is sampled over a grid of coherence
times and Fourier transformed with respect to $t_1$ and $t_3$.
Instead, we perform these transforms analytically at the level of the
Liouvillian propagator. Using the Fourier convention
%
\begin{equation}
\int_0^\infty
dt\,
e^{i\omega t}
e^{\mathcal{L}t},
\end{equation}
%
we define the causal Liouvillian resolvent
%
\begin{equation}
G(\omega)
=
\left[
(0^{+}-i\omega)I-\mathcal{L}
\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:resolvent}
\end{equation}
%
where $0^{+}$ specifies the retarded boundary condition. Equivalently,
%
\begin{equation}
G(\omega)
=
\int_0^\infty
dt\,
e^{i\omega t}
e^{\mathcal{L}t}.
\label{eq:resolvent_integral}
\end{equation}

We then define the mixed frequency--time third-order molecular response
as
%
\begin{equation}
M^{(3)}(\omega_3,T,\omega_1)
=
\int_0^\infty dt_3
\int_0^\infty dt_1\,
e^{i\omega_3 t_3}
e^{i\omega_1 t_1}
R^{(3)}(t_3,T,t_1).
\label{eq:mixed_response_definition}
\end{equation}
%
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:third_order_time} and applying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:resolvent_integral} to the $t_1$ and $t_3$ propagators
gives
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
M^{(3)}(\omega_3,T,\omega_1)
=
\langle\!\langle \mu |
G(\omega_3)
V
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
V
G(\omega_1)
V
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle
}
\label{eq:direct_response}
\end{equation}
%
which is the central direct frequency-domain molecular response used
throughout this work.

Equation~\eqref{eq:direct_response} eliminates the need to propagate
the system over a two-dimensional $(t_1,t_3)$ grid and subsequently
Fourier transform the result. Instead, the response is evaluated
directly at the requested spectral coordinates
$(\omega_1,\omega_3)$. The waiting-time dynamics remain explicitly
contained in $e^{\mathcal{L}T}$, so that relaxation, population
transfer, and coherent evolution during $T$ are retained.

In numerical calculations, the action of the resolvent need not be
obtained by explicitly constructing the matrix inverse in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:resolvent}. For a Liouville-space vector
$|b\rangle\rangle$, one may instead solve
%
\begin{equation}
\left[
(0^{+}-i\omega)I-\mathcal{L}
\right]
|x\rangle\rangle
=
|b\rangle\rangle ,
\label{eq:resolvent_linear_system}
\end{equation}
%
with
$|x\rangle\rangle=G(\omega)|b\rangle\rangle$.
The implementation and reuse of these frequency-dependent solves are
discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:software}.

\subsection{Impulsive and Rotating-Wave Approximations}
\label{sec:impulsive_rwa}

Equation~\eqref{eq:direct_response} is written in terms of the complete
dipole interaction superoperator $V$ and therefore contains all
excitation-raising and excitation-lowering interaction sequences
generated by the dipole commutator. In the impulsive limit, the three
field interactions are assumed to occur at sharply defined times, so
that the temporal pulse envelopes are not resolved explicitly.

To recover the conventional rotating-wave description of phase-matched
multidimensional signals, we decompose the molecular dipole operator as
%
\begin{equation}
\mu
=
\mu_{\uparrow}
+
\mu_{\downarrow},
\qquad
\mu_{\downarrow}
=
\mu_{\uparrow}^{\dagger},
\label{eq:dipole_decomposition}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mu_{\uparrow}$ and $\mu_{\downarrow}$ increase and decrease the
excitation number, respectively. The corresponding interaction
superoperators are
%
\begin{equation}
V_{\uparrow}
=
\frac{i}{\hbar}
\left(
I\otimes\mu_{\uparrow}
-
\mu_{\uparrow}^{T}\otimes I
\right),
\label{eq:V_up}
\end{equation}
%
and
%
\begin{equation}
V_{\downarrow}
=
\frac{i}{\hbar}
\left(
I\otimes\mu_{\downarrow}
-
\mu_{\downarrow}^{T}\otimes I
\right).
\label{eq:V_down}
\end{equation}
%
Thus,
%
\begin{equation}
V
=
V_{\uparrow}
+
V_{\downarrow}.
\label{eq:V_decomposition}
\end{equation}

For compactness, we introduce the notation
%
\begin{equation}
V_{+}
\equiv
V_{\uparrow},
\qquad
V_{-}
\equiv
V_{\downarrow}.
\label{eq:V_pm}
\end{equation}
%
Within the rotating-wave approximation (RWA), the interaction sequence
is restricted according to the selected optical phase signature.
This pathway decomposition is the conventional basis for separating
rephasing, nonrephasing, and related phase-matched multidimensional
signals
\cite{
Hybl1998TwoDimensional,
Abramavicius2009CoherentMultidimensional}.

For the nonrephasing (NR) branch, the RWA signature is
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{NR}}
=
(+,-,+),
\label{eq:NR_signature}
\end{equation}
%
giving the impulsive molecular response
%
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{NR}}^{(3)}
(\omega_3,T,\omega_1)
=
\langle\!\langle\mu|
G(\omega_3)
V_{+}
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
V_{-}
G(\omega_1)
V_{+}
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle .
\label{eq:NR_impulsive_rwa}
\end{equation}

For the rephasing (R) branch, the RWA signature is
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{R}}
=
(-,+,+).
\label{eq:R_signature}
\end{equation}
%
Under the Fourier convention of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mixed_response_definition}, the first coherence frequency
of the rephasing branch appears at negative frequency. Denoting this
signed frequency by $\nu_1<0$, the corresponding response is
%
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{R}}^{(3)}
(\omega_3,T,\nu_1)
=
\langle\!\langle\mu|
G(\omega_3)
V_{+}
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
V_{+}
G(\nu_1)
V_{-}
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle .
\label{eq:R_impulsive_rwa}
\end{equation}

More generally, for an RWA interaction signature
$\mathbf{s}=(s_1,s_2,s_3)$, with $s_j\in\{+,-\}$, we write
%
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
(\omega_3,T,\omega_1)
=
\langle\!\langle\mu|
G(\omega_3)
V_{s_3}
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
V_{s_2}
G(\omega_1)
V_{s_1}
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle .
\label{eq:general_impulsive_rwa}
\end{equation}

The complete interaction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:V_decomposition} contains
interaction sequences that are discarded by the RWA. This distinction
will be used below to define a controlled hierarchy of approximations:
the full finite-pulse response retains the complete $V$, whereas the
impulsive RWA response of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_impulsive_rwa} constitutes the most reduced
description considered in this work.

\subsection{Finite-Pulse Liouvillian Dressing}
\label{sec:finite_pulse}

We now include the temporal envelopes and carrier frequencies of the
three laser pulses. For pulse $j$, we introduce the local time coordinate
%
\begin{equation}
u_j = t-\tau_j,
\end{equation}
%
where $\tau_j$ is the pulse-center time. The real electric field is
decomposed into positive- and negative-frequency components,
%
\begin{equation}
E_j(u_j)
=
E_j^{(+)}(u_j)
+
E_j^{(-)}(u_j),
\end{equation}
%
with
%
\begin{equation}
E_j^{(s)}(u)
=
\frac{E_{0j}}{2}
\exp\left(
-\frac{u^2}{2\sigma_j^2}
\right)
e^{is\Phi_j}
e^{-is\omega_{Lj}u},
\qquad
s=\pm 1.
\label{eq:gaussian_field_component}
\end{equation}
%
Here, $\sigma_j$ is the temporal Gaussian width,
$\omega_{Lj}$ is the carrier frequency, and
$\Phi_j=\mathbf{k}_j\cdot\mathbf{R}+\phi_j$ contains the spatial and
carrier phases.

Using the Fourier convention of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mixed_response_definition}, the corresponding scalar
laser spectrum is
%
\begin{equation}
E_j^{(s)}(\omega)
=
C_j e^{is\Phi_j}
\exp\left[
-\frac{\sigma_j^2}{2}
\left(
\omega-s\omega_{Lj}
\right)^2
\right],
\label{eq:gaussian_spectrum}
\end{equation}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}
C_j
=
\frac{E_{0j}}{2}
\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_j.
\label{eq:pulse_prefactor}
\end{equation}

For finite-width pulses, the light--matter interactions may occur away
from the nominal pulse centers. We continue to assume that the pulses
are sufficiently separated that their chronological ordering is well
defined. When pulse envelopes overlap appreciably, additional temporal
orderings and response pathways can contribute and must in general be
treated explicitly
\cite{RoseKrich2021DiagramGenerator}. In particular, the causality factor associated with the second
and third interactions is approximated as
%
\begin{equation}
\Theta(T+u_3-u_2)
\simeq
1
\label{eq:separated_pulse_condition}
\end{equation}
%
over the region in which the pulse envelopes have appreciable weight.
This separated-pulse approximation does not neglect molecular evolution
during the pulse durations.

Previous finite-pulse formulations have shown that pulse-envelope
effects can enter the nonlinear response in ways that depend on the
molecular pathway and dynamics
\cite{
Smallwood2017FinitePulse,
Perlik2017FinitePulse,
DoGelinTan2017FinitePulse}.

Retaining that evolution leads naturally to operator-valued pulse
factors. For a field-sign signature
$\mathbf{s}=(s_1,s_2,s_3)$, we define
%
\begin{align}
F_1^{(s_1)}(\omega_1)
&=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
du_1\,
e^{i\omega_1u_1}
E_1^{(s_1)}(u_1)
e^{\mathcal{L}u_1},
\label{eq:F1}
\\
F_2^{(s_2)}(\omega_1)
&=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
du_2\,
e^{-i\omega_1u_2}
E_2^{(s_2)}(u_2)
e^{-\mathcal{L}u_2},
\label{eq:F2}
\\
F_3^{(s_3)}(\omega_3)
&=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
du_3\,
e^{i\omega_3u_3}
E_3^{(s_3)}(u_3)
e^{\mathcal{L}u_3}.
\label{eq:F3}
\end{align}
%
Unlike the scalar spectra of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gaussian_spectrum}, the
operators $F_j$ are matrix functions of the field-free Liouvillian and
therefore act directly on populations and coherences in Liouville
space.

For Gaussian pulses, the integrations can be carried out analytically,
giving
%
\begin{align}
F_1^{(s)}(\omega_1)
&=
C_1 e^{is\Phi_1}
\exp\left[
\frac{\sigma_1^2}{2}
\left(
\mathcal{L}
+
i(\omega_1-s\omega_{L1})I
\right)^2
\right],
\label{eq:F1_gaussian}
\\
F_2^{(s)}(\omega_1)
&=
C_2 e^{is\Phi_2}
\exp\left[
\frac{\sigma_2^2}{2}
\left(
\mathcal{L}
+
i(\omega_1+s\omega_{L2})I
\right)^2
\right],
\label{eq:F2_gaussian}
\\
F_3^{(s)}(\omega_3)
&=
C_3 e^{is\Phi_3}
\exp\left[
\frac{\sigma_3^2}{2}
\left(
\mathcal{L}
+
i(\omega_3-s\omega_{L3})I
\right)^2
\right].
\label{eq:F3_gaussian}
\end{align}
%
Thus, finite-pulse effects appear as analytic Liouvillian-dependent
spectral filters. Although the three $F_j$ are functions of the same
Liouvillian and therefore commute with one another,
they do not generally commute with the light--matter interaction,
%
\begin{equation}
[F_j,V]\neq 0.
\label{eq:FV_noncommute}
\end{equation}
%
Their positions within the response therefore cannot be rearranged
arbitrarily.

Retaining both the finite-pulse operators and the complete molecular
interaction defines the most general separated-pulse model considered
here,
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P_{A,\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
=
\langle\!\langle\mu|
G(\omega_3)
V
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
F_3^{(s_3)}
F_2^{(s_2)}
V
G(\omega_1)
V
F_1^{(s_1)}
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle
}
\label{eq:model_A}
\end{equation}
%
where the frequency arguments of the $F_j$ are those defined in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:F1}--\eqref{eq:F3}. We refer to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:model_A} as \emph{Model A}. The field signs select the
phase-matched field contribution, while the complete interaction
superoperator $V=V_{+}+V_{-}$ is retained at each molecular
interaction.

To isolate the effect of the rotating-wave approximation while
retaining the same finite-pulse dressing, we additionally define
\emph{Model B} by restricting each molecular interaction according to
the field signature,
%
\begin{equation}
P_{B,\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
=
\langle\!\langle\mu|
G(\omega_3)
V_{s_3}
e^{\mathcal{L}T}
F_3^{(s_3)}
F_2^{(s_2)}
V_{s_2}
G(\omega_1)
V_{s_1}
F_1^{(s_1)}
|\rho_0\rangle\!\rangle .
\label{eq:model_B}
\end{equation}

A further reduction is obtained when the molecular evolution across the
pulse-centered time offsets can be neglected. In this limit,
%
\begin{align}
F_1^{(s_1)}(\omega_1)
&\rightarrow
E_1^{(s_1)}(\omega_1)I,
\\
F_2^{(s_2)}(\omega_1)
&\rightarrow
E_2^{(s_2)}(-\omega_1)I,
\\
F_3^{(s_3)}(\omega_3)
&\rightarrow
E_3^{(s_3)}(\omega_3)I.
\end{align}
%
The polarization then factorizes into scalar pulse spectra and the
impulsive RWA molecular response,
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P_{C,\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
=
E_3^{(s_3)}(\omega_3)
E_2^{(s_2)}(-\omega_1)
E_1^{(s_1)}(\omega_1)
M_{\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
(\omega_3,T,\omega_1)
}
\label{eq:model_C}
\end{equation}
%
which we call \emph{Model C}. This is the short, separated-pulse RWA
description.

Finally, removing the finite spectral weighting gives the impulsive RWA
response
%
\begin{equation}
P_{D,\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
\equiv
M_{\mathbf{s}}^{(3)}
(\omega_3,T,\omega_1),
\label{eq:model_D}
\end{equation}
%
which defines \emph{Model D} and corresponds to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_impulsive_rwa}.

The resulting sequence
%
\begin{equation}
A
\;\longrightarrow\;
B
\;\longrightarrow\;
C
\;\longrightarrow\;
D
\label{eq:approximation_hierarchy}
\end{equation}
%
forms a controlled approximation hierarchy. The $A\rightarrow B$
step introduces the RWA, $B\rightarrow C$ neglects the molecular
evolution occurring across the finite pulse envelopes, and
$C\rightarrow D$ removes the finite spectral bandwidth of the pulses.
This hierarchy will be used below to identify which approximation
dominates the error in different spectroscopic regimes.

\subsection{Physical Regimes and Dimensionless Parameters}
\label{sec:physical_regimes}

The approximation hierarchy of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:approximation_hierarchy} is governed by distinct
timescale comparisons. In particular, validity of the RWA and validity
of the short-pulse approximation are not equivalent requirements.

The importance of molecular evolution during a pulse may be
characterized by the dimensionless parameter
%
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}
=
\sigma\Omega_{\mathrm{dyn}},
\label{eq:chi_dyn}
\end{equation}
%
where $\sigma$ is a characteristic Gaussian field width and
$\Omega_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ is a characteristic molecular frequency
associated with the dynamics retained after the optical carrier has
been removed. When
%
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}\ll 1,
\end{equation}
%
the molecular state changes only weakly over the pulse duration, and
the operator-valued factors $F_j$ approach the scalar pulse spectra
used in Model C. Increasing $\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ enhances the
importance of the Liouvillian dressing retained in Models A and B.

A separate dimensionless scale characterizes the rotating-wave
approximation. Counter-rotating terms oscillate with frequencies of
order
%
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{cr}}
\sim
\Omega+\omega_L,
\end{equation}
%
where $\Omega$ is an optical transition frequency and $\omega_L$ is
the laser carrier frequency. Near resonance,
$\Omega\simeq\omega_L$, giving the approximate scale
%
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{RWA}}
\equiv
2\sigma\omega_L .
\label{eq:chi_rwa}
\end{equation}
%
Large $\chi_{\mathrm{RWA}}$ corresponds to many carrier oscillations
within the pulse envelope and favors dynamical suppression of
counter-rotating contributions, whereas sufficiently small
$\chi_{\mathrm{RWA}}$ indicates that the RWA may fail
\cite{Smallwood2017FinitePulse}.

The conventional short-pulse RWA description therefore occupies an
intermediate timescale regime that can be summarized schematically as
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}\ll 1,
\qquad
\chi_{\mathrm{RWA}}\gg 1 .
}
\label{eq:short_rwa_window}
\end{equation}
%
These inequalities are intended as physical regime indicators rather
than universal numerical thresholds. The quantitative error depends on
the molecular spectrum, dipole couplings, relaxation rates, pulse
carrier frequencies, and the particular spectral region being probed.

For the coupled dimer considered below, a natural molecular dynamical
scale is the single-excitation splitting
%
\begin{equation}
\Delta E_{\mathrm{ex}}
=
\sqrt{
(E_a-E_b)^2+4J^2
},
\label{eq:exciton_splitting}
\end{equation}
%
so that
%
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}
=
\frac{
\sigma\Delta E_{\mathrm{ex}}
}{
\hbar
}.
\label{eq:chi_dyn_dimer}
\end{equation}
%
This quantity provides a convenient dimensionless indicator of
finite-pulse molecular evolution in the regime analysis.

To compare successive levels of the approximation hierarchy, we use
the full complex rephasing and nonrephasing spectra. For two models
$X$ and $Y$, with $X$ taken as the more complete description, we
define the combined relative discrepancy
%
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{XY}^{\mathrm{comb}}
=
\frac{
\sqrt{
\left\|
S_Y^{\mathrm{NR}}-S_X^{\mathrm{NR}}
\right\|_F^2
+
\left\|
S_Y^{\mathrm{R}}-S_X^{\mathrm{R}}
\right\|_F^2
}
}{
\sqrt{
\left\|
S_X^{\mathrm{NR}}
\right\|_F^2
+
\left\|
S_X^{\mathrm{R}}
\right\|_F^2
}
},
\label{eq:combined_error}
\end{equation}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}
\|S\|_F
=
\left(
\sum_{mn}
|S_{mn}|^2
\right)^{1/2}
\label{eq:frobenius_norm}
\end{equation}
%
is the Frobenius norm.

Within the hierarchy,
$\epsilon_{AB}^{\mathrm{comb}}$ isolates the error introduced by the
RWA,
$\epsilon_{BC}^{\mathrm{comb}}$ measures the error associated with
neglecting in-pulse molecular dynamics,
$\epsilon_{CD}^{\mathrm{comb}}$ measures the effect of removing the
finite pulse bandwidth, and
$\epsilon_{AD}^{\mathrm{comb}}$ gives the total discrepancy of the
impulsive-RWA description relative to the full finite-pulse result.
