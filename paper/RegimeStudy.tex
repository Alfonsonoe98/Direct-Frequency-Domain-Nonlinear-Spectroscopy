\section{Breakdown of the Impulsive Rotating-Wave Approximation}
\label{sec:regime_study}

\subsection{Coupled Dimer Model}
\label{subsec:dimer}

To examine the breakdown of the impulsive rotating-wave description,
we consider a minimal coupled electronic dimer consisting of two
two-level chromophores, labeled $a$ and $b$.
In terms of local excitation operators $a_a$ and $a_b$, the electronic
Hamiltonian is
%
\begin{equation}
H
=
E_a a_a^\dagger a_a
+
E_b a_b^\dagger a_b
+
J\left(
a_a^\dagger a_b
+
a_b^\dagger a_a
\right),
\label{eq:dimer_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
%
where $E_a$ and $E_b$ are the site excitation energies and $J$ is the
electronic coupling.
The local operators are restricted to the two-level site Hilbert
spaces, so that the complete model contains the ground,
singly excited, and doubly excited manifolds.

For the calculations below, the site energies are
%
\begin{equation}
E_a = 1.98~\mathrm{eV},
\qquad
E_b = 2.02~\mathrm{eV},
\label{eq:dimer_site_energies}
\end{equation}
%
giving a site-energy detuning of $40~\mathrm{meV}$.
Within the singly excited manifold, the excitonic splitting is
%
\begin{equation}
\Delta E_{\mathrm{ex}}
=
\sqrt{
(E_a-E_b)^2+4J^2
},
\label{eq:dimer_splitting}
\end{equation}
%
which gives
$\Delta E_{\mathrm{ex}}=89.4~\mathrm{meV}$
for the representative value $J=40~\mathrm{meV}$.

The dipole operator is taken to couple each site with equal transition
strength,
%
\begin{equation}
\mu
=
\mu_0
\left(
a_a+a_a^\dagger
+
a_b+a_b^\dagger
\right),
\label{eq:dimer_dipole}
\end{equation}
%
where the overall factor $\mu_0$ sets only the signal scale and is
therefore taken as unity.
For calculations employing the RWA, the corresponding
positive- and negative-frequency components are obtained directly from
the excitation-raising and excitation-lowering terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:dimer_dipole}.

Dissipation is described by independent local Lindblad relaxation
channels with rate
%
\begin{equation}
\gamma = 0.020~\mathrm{fs}^{-1},
\label{eq:dimer_gamma}
\end{equation}
%
and zero thermal occupation.
The initial state is the electronic ground state.

All three laser pulses are taken to be identical, transform-limited
Gaussians centered at
%
\begin{equation}
\hbar\omega_L = 2.00~\mathrm{eV},
\end{equation}
%
with zero carrier phase.
We specify the pulse duration by its intensity full width at half
maximum,
%
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}
=
2\sqrt{\ln 2}\,\sigma ,
\label{eq:intensity_fwhm}
\end{equation}
%
where $\sigma$ is the Gaussian field-envelope width introduced in
Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}.
The waiting time is fixed at
%
\begin{equation}
T=100~\mathrm{fs}.
\end{equation}

To map the approximation regimes, we vary the electronic coupling over
%
\begin{equation}
0\le J\le100~\mathrm{meV}
\end{equation}
%
and the pulse intensity FWHM over
%
\begin{equation}
0.1\le\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}\le30~\mathrm{fs}.
\end{equation}
%
For each point in this parameter space, Models A--D are evaluated on
the same spectral grid and compared using the combined rephasing and
nonrephasing error metric defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}.


\subsection{Finite-Pulse Dynamical Effects}
\label{subsec:pulse_dynamics}

The $B-C$ comparison isolates the error introduced by neglecting
molecular evolution during the finite duration of the laser pulses.
Both models employ the rotating-wave approximation and retain the
finite spectral bandwidth of the Gaussian pulses, but they differ in
the treatment of molecular dynamics across the pulse envelopes.
Model B retains the Liouvillian dressing operators
$F_j(\mathcal{L},\omega)$, whereas Model C replaces them by scalar
spectral factors.

The importance of this approximation is controlled by the amount of
field-free evolution that can occur during a pulse.
For the coupled dimer, a natural dimensionless measure is
%
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}
=
\frac{\sigma\Delta E_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\hbar},
\label{eq:dimer_chi_dyn}
\end{equation}
%
where $\Delta E_{\mathrm{ex}}$ is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:dimer_splitting}.
When $\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}\ll1$, the system evolves only weakly over the
pulse envelope and the short-pulse approximation is expected to be
accurate.
As either the pulse duration or the excitonic splitting increases,
molecular evolution during the pulse becomes progressively more
important and the difference between Models B and C grows.

This behavior is confirmed by the numerical scan over $J$ and pulse
duration.
For the parameter range considered here, the combined $B-C$
discrepancy spans from below $10^{-3}\%$ in the shortest-pulse regime
to approximately $46\%$ for the longest pulses and largest splittings.
The variation is smooth rather than associated with a sharp threshold,
so $\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ should be regarded as a regime indicator rather
than as a universal predictor of a particular percentage error.

Importantly, this contribution is distinct from ordinary finite
bandwidth.
Model C still retains the Gaussian spectral envelope of each pulse;
the $B-C$ difference therefore reflects the dynamics generated by
$\mathcal{L}$ during the pulse interaction itself.
The separate $C-D$ comparison measures the additional effect of
removing the finite pulse bandwidth altogether.


\subsection{Non-Rotating-Wave Contributions}
\label{subsec:nonrwa}

The $A-B$ comparison isolates the effect of the rotating-wave
approximation while retaining the same finite-pulse treatment.
Model A uses the complete interaction superoperator $V$, whereas
Model B retains only the resonant raising- and lowering-frequency
components associated with the selected spectroscopic pathway.
For the nonrephasing signal, the conventional RWA contribution has the
field-sign signature
%
\begin{equation}
(+,-,+).
\label{eq:nr_rwa_signature}
\end{equation}

When the complete interaction is retained, the finite-pulse
third-order response can be decomposed into all eight combinations
%
\begin{equation}
(s_1,s_2,s_3),
\qquad
s_j\in\{-1,+1\}.
\label{eq:all_signatures}
\end{equation}
%
The full Model-A nonrephasing response may therefore be written as
%
\begin{equation}
S_A^{\mathrm{NR}}
=
\sum_{s_1,s_2,s_3=\pm1}
S_{s_1s_2s_3}^{(3)},
\label{eq:nr_signature_sum}
\end{equation}
%
while Model B retains only the $(+,-,+)$ sector.
This decomposition is evaluated directly using the arbitrary
field-sign interface described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:software_signatures}.

A useful qualitative measure of the expected RWA validity is the
dimensionless carrier-scale parameter
%
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{RWA}}
\sim
2\sigma\omega_L ,
\label{eq:dimer_chi_rwa}
\end{equation}
%
introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}.
For long pulses, the rapidly oscillating counter-rotating contributions
are strongly suppressed and the RWA becomes accurate.
For sufficiently short pulses, this averaging is incomplete and
additional field-sign sectors can contribute appreciably.
As with $\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}$, however,
$\chi_{\mathrm{RWA}}$ is only a timescale indicator and does not by
itself determine the magnitude of the spectroscopic error.

The numerical decomposition shows that the beyond-RWA correction is
not distributed uniformly among the discarded sectors.
For short pulses, the largest omitted contribution to the
nonrephasing signal is the
%
\begin{equation}
(+,+,-)
\end{equation}
%
sector.
At a pulse intensity FWHM of $0.10~\mathrm{fs}$, its Frobenius norm is
approximately $37\%$ of the conventional RWA nonrephasing response for
$J=20~\mathrm{meV}$ and approximately $45\%$ for
$J=40~\mathrm{meV}$.
In the uncoupled limit, $J=0$, this contribution vanishes for the model
considered here.

The corresponding spectra and coupling dependence are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:beyond_rwa_mechanism}.
The conventional $(+,-,+)$ and discarded $(+,+,-)$ sectors occupy
similar regions of the two-dimensional spectrum, but the latter is
absent in the RWA and grows rapidly with electronic coupling.
The short-pulse RWA breakdown therefore reflects a coupling-enabled
spectroscopic pathway rather than a uniform rescaling of the
conventional RWA spectrum.
More generally, the RWA error depends not only on the pulse duration
relative to the optical carrier period, but also on the molecular
couplings that determine how discarded field-sign sectors connect the
available Liouville-space pathways.


\subsection{Spectroscopic Regime Map}
\label{subsec:regime_map}

The separate comparisons above can be combined into a single map of
the approximation hierarchy.
For each electronic coupling $J$ and pulse duration
$\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}$, we evaluate Models A--D and compute the
combined rephasing and nonrephasing discrepancy defined in
Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}.
The four comparisons
%
\begin{equation}
A-B,\qquad
B-C,\qquad
C-D,\qquad
A-D
\end{equation}
%
therefore quantify, respectively, the RWA error, the error from
neglecting in-pulse molecular dynamics, the finite-bandwidth
contribution, and the total error of the impulsive-RWA description.

Across the parameter range considered here, the dominant source of
error changes with pulse duration.
For extremely short pulses, the finite-bandwidth correction is small,
but the rotating-wave approximation can fail because counter-rotating
field-sign sectors are no longer efficiently averaged out.
At intermediate pulse durations, both the RWA and in-pulse dynamical
errors can be simultaneously small, producing a region in which the
impulsive-RWA description closely reproduces the full finite-pulse
response.
For longer pulses, molecular evolution during the pulse envelopes and
the finite spectral bandwidth become increasingly important.

The total $A-D$ discrepancy therefore exhibits a nonmonotonic
dependence on pulse duration.
For the present dimer, the smallest combined discrepancy found in the
two-dimensional scan is approximately
%
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{AD}^{\mathrm{comb}}
\simeq 0.93\%,
\end{equation}
%
occurring near
%
\begin{equation}
J\simeq56~\mathrm{meV},
\qquad
\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}\simeq0.70~\mathrm{fs}.
\end{equation}
%
This minimum lies between the short-pulse regime dominated by
non-RWA contributions and the long-pulse regime dominated by
finite-pulse molecular dynamics and bandwidth.

The resulting regime map is summarized in
Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map}.
Contours at $1\%$, $5\%$, and $10\%$ provide convenient visual guides
to the accuracy of each approximation.
These boundaries are specific to the present molecular model and
error metric; they should not be interpreted as universal thresholds.

The low-error region in Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map}(d) forms a narrow
intermediate-pulse-duration window rather than extending monotonically
toward the impulsive limit.
At shorter durations the RWA error rises rapidly, whereas at longer
durations the $B-C$ and $C-D$ contributions increase.
The resulting minimum therefore reflects a balance between physically
distinct approximations rather than the vanishing of a single error
source.

To visualize these regimes at the level of the spectrum,
Fig.~\ref{fig:representative_spectra} shows pointwise differences
between the full finite-pulse response (Model A) and the
impulsive-RWA result (Model D) at fixed
$J=40~\mathrm{meV}$.
Three pulse durations sample the short-pulse, intermediate, and
long-pulse regimes identified in Fig.~\ref{fig:regime_map}.

At $\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}=0.10~\mathrm{fs}$, the nonrephasing response
is strongly affected, with a total relative discrepancy of
$42.20\%$, whereas the rephasing response remains close to the
full calculation at $0.99\%$.
At the intermediate duration
$\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}=0.70~\mathrm{fs}$, both pathways lie in the
high-accuracy region, with discrepancies of approximately $0.94\%$.
By contrast, at
$\tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}=20~\mathrm{fs}$, the impulsive approximation
fails strongly for both pathways, giving discrepancies of
$161.25\%$ and $153.98\%$ for NR and R, respectively.
The total approximation error is therefore both nonmonotonic in pulse
duration and pathway dependent.

Taken together, Figs.~\ref{fig:regime_map} and
\ref{fig:representative_spectra} show that there is no single
``short-pulse'' limit in which all approximations improve
simultaneously.
Reducing the pulse duration suppresses in-pulse molecular evolution
and broadens the available bandwidth, but eventually exposes
counter-rotating contributions that are removed by the RWA.
The intermediate high-accuracy region therefore results from the
competition between these distinct physical limits.
